In this lab session, we are transforming a corpus of inaugural speeches of US Presidents into a Document-Feature-matrix. We are not yet worrying about creating a corpus - that will come when we talk about finding and importing your own texts. This corpus comes included with quanteda and can be loaded just by assigning it to a variable:

library(quanteda)
speeches <- data_corpus_inaugural

We will use some functions that were not covered in the lecture. Most of the tasks we are applying here can be found in the quanteda tutorial by Müller and Watanabe.

Tokenization

  1. Take the 21st century corpus (with the documents being full speeches). Tokenize it while removing punctuation. Then remove all English stopwords as well. Compare the length of the tokenized speeches to the number of tokens you got in 2).
toks_21 <- tokens(speeches_21,
                  remove_punct = T) |> 
  tokens_remove(stopwords("en"))

ntoken(toks_21)
 2001-Bush  2005-Bush 2009-Obama 2013-Obama 2017-Trump 2021-Biden 2025-Trump 
       784       1041       1173       1036        715       1133       1452

Every speech loses a bit more than half its tokens when applying basic pre-processing steps. This shows just how much of a speech is punctuation and stop words!

  1. Take any instance of “United States” in the speeches and compound the token to “United_States”.
toks_21 <- tokens_compound(toks_21,
                           pattern = phrase("United States"))

  1. Create a document-feature-matrix of all speeches in the corpus. How do the 10 most common features across all years differ from the 21st century corpus?
dfmat_all <- speeches |>
  tokens(remove_punct = T) |> 
  tokens_remove(stopwords("en")) |> 
  tokens_compound(
    pattern = phrase("United States")
  ) |> 
  dfm() |> 
  dfm_trim(min_termfreq = 10)

topfeatures(dfmat_all, n = 10)
    people government         us        can       must       upon      great 
       592        575        507        489        377        371        354 
       may      world     nation 
       343        329        324

“People” and “Government” stand out as being much more common, as is “World”. On the other hand, “Nation” becomes less relevant compared to 21st century speeches. Based on these top features alone, we might suspect that speeches have become more patriotic or even nationalist, compared to being focused more on the American people and their place in the world. However, drawing such conclusions from just 10 features would of course be very overly ambitious!
